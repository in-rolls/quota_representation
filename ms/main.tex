\begin{abstract}
\noindent A central promise of electoral gender quotas is that they will help women win office after the quotas end. By demonstrating women's competence, reducing prejudice, and expanding the pool of experienced women politicians, quotas may create lasting electoral opportunities. We test this promise using quota assignment across four election cycles spanning sixteen years in more than \abstractGpCount{} rural local bodies in India. We distinguish effects in the next open election, their duration across later open elections, and the effects of additional reserved terms. The average next-election gain is \poolEstimate{} percentage points (95\% interval [\poolLow{}, \poolHigh{}]), against a non-quota win rate of \poolControl{}\%. Across later open elections, we find no consistent pattern of growth or decay. Holding the most recent reserved term fixed, a second term adds an estimated $\twoTermExtraMin{}$ to \twoTermExtraMax{} points. Three reserved terms rather than one yield estimated gains of \upFullExtra{}--\rajFullExtra{} points, but these gains shrink to near zero and become imprecise in districts where tests do not detect dependence between earlier quota assignments. Male relatives' mediation of access to women officeholders may help explain these limited electoral gains. In a phone audit of roughly \abstractPhoneN{} representatives, we reached the elected officeholder in \phoneQuotaMemberWhole{}\% of quota seats with an answered call, compared with \phoneOpenMemberWhole{}\% of non-quota seats. Women elected under quotas are also younger, less educated, and less wealthy than non-quota winners. These patterns suggest that holding office may offer women limited opportunities to build independent political careers.

\vspace{6mm}

\noindent \textbf{Keywords:} Women in Politics, Gender Quotas, India, Local Government

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Who holds office affects whose interests government serves. In India, reserving local offices for women has shifted public spending toward women's priorities (\citealp{chattopadhyay2004women}; cf.\ \citealp{ban2008tokenism, gajwani2015gender, green2026allocative}). Women's political representation has also increased the reporting of crimes against women \citep{iyer2012power}, while exposure to women leaders has raised girls' aspirations and educational attainment \citep{beaman2012female}. These instrumental benefits complement the democratic case for women having an equal voice in government. Quotas provide one route to greater representation, with the promise that their effects will extend beyond the reserved term. By demonstrating women's competence, reducing prejudice, and giving women political experience and networks, quotas may increase both voter support for women and the pool of viable women candidates. A central question is whether these changes help women win office after reservation ends.

Evidence on electoral success after quotas end is mixed. Temporary candidate-list quotas in Italy produced modest increases in women's representation after repeal \citep{de2010can}, while estimates for village leadership in West Bengal are small or imprecise \citep{beaman2009powerful}. A larger estimate comes from Mumbai, where women were \appMumbaiEstimate{} percentage points more likely to win an unreserved seat following reservation, in a study of \appMumbaiN{} wards \citep{bhavnani2009electoral}. These findings leave unresolved how broadly such gains extend, whether they endure beyond the next election, and whether additional reserved terms strengthen them.

We examine these questions using sixteen years of electoral data from \linkedGpCount{} Gram Panchayats across four election cycles in Rajasthan and Uttar Pradesh (with additional rural and urban evidence in Appendix~\ref{sec:additional_evidence}). A constitutional mandate reserves at least one third of local government leadership positions for women (Constitution of India, Article~243D). We exploit quota assignment to distinguish three questions: whether reservation helps women win the next open election, how that effect changes across subsequent open elections, and what additional reserved terms contribute.

We find a small average gain in women's electoral success in the next open election. Giving equal weight to each state and election transition yields \poolEstimate{} percentage points (95\% interval [\poolLow{}, \poolHigh{}]), against an equally weighted non-quota win rate of \poolControl{}\%. A meta-analysis combining these results with evidence from Mumbai, Delhi, Chandigarh, Bengaluru, and West Bengal yields an estimated average gain of \appMetaEstimate{} percentage points (95\% interval \appMetaInterval{}). We find no clear evidence that effects are lower in rural than in urban settings (Appendix~\ref{sec:additional_rural_urban}).

We also examine whether electoral gains persist beyond the first open election. Following the same places through successive open elections, estimates remain small in UP ($\persistUpSecond{}$ to \persistUpDoubleFirst{} percentage points) and vary widely in Rajasthan ($\persistRajDoubleFirst{}$ to \persistRajLaterSecond{} points). We find no consistent pattern of growth or decay after reservation ends (Appendix~\ref{sec:persistence_horizons}).

\par
We also ask whether additional reserved terms produce larger electoral gains. Holding the most recent reserved term fixed, a second term adds an estimated $\twoTermExtraMin{}$ to \twoTermExtraMax{} percentage points, with all 95\% confidence intervals including zero. Three terms rather than one yield larger estimates: \rajFullExtra{} points in Rajasthan and \upFullExtra{} in UP. But when we limit the analysis to districts where tests do not detect dependence between earlier quota assignments, these estimates are near zero and imprecise: $\rajRestrictedExtra{}$ points in Rajasthan (95\% interval [\rajRestrictedExtraLow{}, \rajRestrictedExtraHigh{}]) and \upRestrictedExtra{} in UP ([\upRestrictedExtraLow{}, \upRestrictedExtraHigh{}]). The results remain consistent with small gains from additional exposure; the evidence for larger gains after three terms depends on which districts enter the analysis.

Male relatives' mediation of access to women officeholders may help explain why holding office does not translate more fully into subsequent electoral success. In a phone audit covering roughly \abstractPhoneN{} representatives in Rajasthan, we reached the elected officeholder in \phoneQuotaMemberWhole{}\% of quota seats with an answered call, compared with \phoneOpenMemberWhole{}\% of non-quota seats. Women elected under quotas are also younger, less educated, and less wealthy than non-quota winners. They are less likely to reappear as candidates in the next election when the seat is open to all genders and castes (Appendix~\ref{sec:recontesting}). These patterns are consistent with quotas drawing women with weaker prior political ties into office, or with officeholding offering them limited opportunities to exercise authority, gain experience, and build independent political careers \citep{valdini2019inclusion, brule2023actually, meguid2025strategic}.

\section{Theoretical Framework}\label{sec:theory}
For quotas to help women win after reservation ends, holding office must change voters' willingness to elect women or women's ability and willingness to contest. One route is through learning about competence. Voters uncertain about women's ability to govern may use gender as a proxy. Seeing a woman manage public funds, deliver services, and respond to constituents can provide evidence against unfavorable beliefs. Exposure to women leaders has reduced bias in assessments of women's effectiveness, although changes in beliefs need not translate into equally large electoral gains \citep{beaman2009powerful}. This mechanism depends on what voters observe and whom they credit for the results.

Exposure may also reduce prejudice against women in authority. Familiarity with women leaders could make female leadership more acceptable even without changing beliefs about competence. This differs from learning that women govern effectively: voters can recognize competence while continuing to prefer men. In West Bengal, exposure reduced bias in assessments of effectiveness without eliminating men's preference for male leaders \citep{beaman2009powerful}. The distinction matters because quotas may change one barrier to electoral support while leaving another intact.

Quotas can also expand the pool of women able and willing to compete. Office can provide political experience, constituent relationships, recognition, and access to networks. These gains may help the elected woman contest again, while her example encourages other women to enter politics. Exposure to women leaders has raised girls' aspirations and educational attainment \citep{beaman2012female}, and women politicians have recruited more women into grassroots party activism \citep{goyal2024representation}. These findings identify routes through which representation could broaden participation; whether they lead to more women contesting and winning later elections remains an empirical question. Returning officeholders and new entrants are distinct sources of any electoral gain.

Holding office need not give women the authority or visibility that these mechanisms require. Male relatives may conduct public business, negotiate with officials, and receive credit for the council's work, a practice commonly described as \textit{sarpanch pati} when the husband assumes this role \citep{thakur2015brothers, brule2023actually}. Such involvement can limit what voters learn about women's competence, what other women see as possible, and the experience officeholders acquire. It can also constrain women's influence over spending and other policy choices. But assistance from a spouse need not eliminate women's agency: women may still influence decisions or make it easier for other women to express their preferences \citep{beaman2011substantive}. The implications depend on how authority and public credit are shared.

Women may also enter office with fewer of the resources needed to govern independently. Winners of reserved seats can have less schooling and political experience than non-quota winners, even when they are positively selected relative to other women \citep{ban2008tokenism}. In Tamil Nadu, female presidents knew less about panchayat rules and had less contact with higher-level officials despite relatively similar schooling; their councils also built fewer schools and roads, which require coordination with those officials \citep{gajwani2015gender}. Differences in qualifications vary across offices and settings \citep{soodQualifications}, and age or schooling alone does not establish effectiveness. Experience can improve performance \citep{ban2008tokenism, afridi2013learning}, but acquiring that experience requires opportunities to participate in governing.

These mechanisms have distinct implications for the timing of electoral gains. Learning, reduced prejudice, and a larger candidate pool could help women in the first election after a quota ends. Gains could endure in later elections if beliefs, networks, and political experience persist. Additional reserved terms could strengthen these changes, although there is no reason to expect each term to contribute equally. Repeated reservation of an office can also elect different women, so it need not provide any one officeholder with additional experience. We therefore distinguish the next-election effect, its duration across later open elections, and the contribution of additional reserved terms.

Limited authority and preparation could weaken each step from electing women to helping women win later. If male relatives perform the public role and receive credit, voters see less evidence of women's leadership, potential candidates see fewer women exercising power, and officeholders have fewer opportunities to build independent political careers. Constraints on administrative knowledge and access to officials may further limit what women accomplish in office. Our phone audit and comparisons of officeholder characteristics help interpret the electoral results in light of these possibilities. The audit records access to the elected representative, while the administrative records describe who enters office. Following winners into the next election provides a separate indication of whether holding office is followed by continued electoral participation (Appendix~\ref{sec:recontesting}).

The strength of these channels may vary across settings. Greater access to education, public institutions, and political networks could make it easier for women to govern independently and build support. Evidence from South India links women's relative performance to political experience and the institutional environment \citep{ban2008tokenism}. Yet settings where women already have more political opportunities may leave less room for quotas to change electoral outcomes. Rural--urban differences therefore provide evidence about variation in quota effects, without identifying which feature of the setting produces that variation. We examine these differences alongside the evidence on persistence (Appendix~\ref{sec:additional_rural_urban}).

\section{Institutional Background}\label{sec:background}

We study the elected heads of Gram Panchayats (GPs) in Rajasthan and Uttar Pradesh. A GP comprises one or more villages and forms the lowest tier of rural local government, below the block and district councils (Figure~\ref{fig:local_govts}). Its head, called the \textit{sarpanch} in Rajasthan and the \textit{pradhan} in UP, is directly elected by the GP's voters. The head leads a council of elected ward members with responsibilities for local development, public services, and the administration of government programs \citep{rajasthan1994act, up1947act}.

India's 73rd Constitutional Amendment established a minimum reservation of one third of panchayat head positions for women \citep[Article 243D(4)]{indiaConstitution}. Rajasthan raised this minimum to 50\% in 2008; UP retained the one-third minimum through the 2021 election \citep{mopr2021reservation}. In both states, women's reservations operate alongside reservations for Scheduled Castes (SC), Scheduled Tribes (ST), and Other Backward Classes (OBC). A position can therefore be reserved for women of a particular caste category. Only women can contest women-reserved positions, but women can also contest positions without a gender reservation, subject to any caste restriction \citep{rajasthan1994act, up1947act}. We call positions reserved for women quota seats and those without a gender reservation non-quota or open seats; either can carry a caste reservation.

State rules determine which GP-head positions are reserved at each election. Rajasthan's election rules provide for the allocation of women's reservations by drawing lots \citep[Rule 9(3)]{rajasthan1994electionrules}. UP's procedures have changed over time. The published account of the 2005 and 2010 elections describes selection from caste-specific serial lists, with changes in caste reservation allowing consecutive reservations for women \citep[p.~206]{bose2018political}. In 2015, following delimitation, UP assigned reservations afresh using population-based rankings within blocks and caste categories, without carrying forward previous reservation assignments \citep[p.~760]{chaturvedi2025quotas}.

Panchayats normally serve five-year terms \citep[Article 243E]{indiaConstitution}. Across elections, a GP-head position may remain reserved for women or become open to women and men. Our panels contain both consecutive reserved terms and reserved terms followed by one or more open elections. These histories allow us to examine women's electoral success after reservation ends and after different lengths of exposure. When a position becomes open, the woman who held it under reservation can compete alongside other eligible candidates; any subsequent increase in women's representation can therefore come from returning officeholders or new entrants.

\section{Research Design and Data}\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Treatment, outcomes, and comparisons}\label{sec:comparisons}\label{sec:duration_design}

We estimate the effect of reserving GP-head positions for women on women's subsequent electoral participation and success. The comparison group consists of positions without a women's reservation, including those won by women. The treatment is therefore quota assignment. Its effects can reflect both changes in who enters office and changes in the electoral opportunities and incentives created by reservation. We retain women elected without quotas in the comparison group and estimate the intent-to-treat effect of reservation \citep{angrist1996identification}.

Our primary outcome is whether a woman wins an election open to both women and men. The next-election comparison asks whether this outcome differs between GPs reserved for women in the preceding election and GPs without that reservation. Both groups must be open in the outcome election, but either may retain a caste reservation. We also examine women's candidacy in Rajasthan's 2020 election, measuring whether any woman contests, the number and proportion of women candidates, and the votes received by women among the winner and runner-up as a share of all valid votes. These outcomes distinguish entry into electoral competition from winning office.

We separate duration from additional exposure to quotas. To study duration, we hold reservation histories and the GP sample fixed and follow women's electoral success across subsequent open elections. The first comparison follows GPs reserved in 2005 and open thereafter against GPs open in all four observed elections. Two further comparisons follow GPs reserved in 2010, with or without reservation in 2005, through two open elections. To study additional exposure, we compare histories at the same final election: for example, two or three prior reserved terms versus reservation only in the immediately preceding term. These comparisons hold the timing of the last reservation fixed while varying earlier reservation. We also compare three prior reserved terms with none during the observed period. Reservation before 2005 is unobserved, so the latter group need not have been free of quotas before our panel begins.

\subsection{Data and analysis samples}\label{sec:analysis_data}

We assemble election records from the Rajasthan and Uttar Pradesh State Election Commissions\footnote{Election records: \url{https://sec.rajasthan.gov.in/} and \url{https://sec.up.nic.in/}.} for four cycles each: 2005, 2010, 2015, and 2020 in Rajasthan, and 2005, 2010, 2015, and 2021 in UP. The records identify the reservation status of the GP-head position and the winner. Winner sex comes from the election records where available; for Rajasthan 2015, it was manually coded from names. Rajasthan's 2020 candidate records supply the candidacy outcomes and officeholder characteristics. We link records within the same election phase and GP, retaining unresolved sex and candidate counts as missing where needed for an outcome. Appendix~\ref{sec:app_data} documents coverage and construction. Separate analyses use additional settings (Appendix~\ref{sec:additional_evidence}) and link winners to subsequent candidates (Appendix~\ref{sec:recontesting}); the phone audit is described in Section~\ref{sec:phone_audit}.

We construct adjacent-election panels by matching GPs within harmonized districts and blocks, resolving spelling differences and administrative renaming. Ambiguous links remain unmatched. Four-election panels join the adjacent links through the same intermediate election records. Coverage varies across years, and matching does not establish that GP boundaries remained unchanged. Table~\ref{tab:next_election_samples} distinguishes the linked panels from the next-election analysis samples, which additionally require an open outcome election, known prior reservation, an observed outcome, and a block identifier. Duration comparisons require the same histories and observed winner sex at every follow-up; their samples are consequently smaller than the adjacent-election samples. The cumulative models require the full observed reservation history and an open final election.

Women won \controlPriorWomenMin{}--\controlPriorWomenMax{}\% of non-quota offices in the treatment election among control GPs with known winner sex (Table~\ref{tab:next_election_samples}). Most control GPs therefore elected men in the treatment election, but the comparison remains between quota and non-quota assignment. These contemporaneous shares describe the control group's composition; women's win rates in the later outcome election provide the baseline for interpreting persistence.

\subsection{Identification}\label{sec:identification}

A causal interpretation requires the compared reservation assignments to be independent of potential electoral outcomes within the relevant allocation groups and analysis sample. Rajasthan's rules specify lotteries for women's reservations, providing the basis for this comparison \citep[Rule 9(3)]{rajasthan1994electionrules}. The eligible offices can depend on caste and reservation history, so a lottery need not produce independent assignments across elections. UP requires separate consideration: the earlier serial-list account and the population rankings used in 2015 describe different procedures \citep{bose2018political, chaturvedi2025quotas}. For UP, the within-block comparisons additionally require that the allocation criteria do not leave differences in potential outcomes between the compared groups. Block fixed effects alone do not establish that condition.

Restricting attention to subsequent open elections creates a further requirement. Past reservation can influence whether a GP becomes open, and additional eligibility rules can affect which histories we observe. Random initial assignment can remain informative under rotation when later eligibility depends only on prior assignments and the controlled allocation groups, with both comparison histories possible. If eligibility also depends on other predictors of women's electoral success, the selected groups can differ for reasons beyond quota assignment. Linkage and missing outcomes can create similar selection. The longer-history comparisons require these conditions over more elections. The policy effect includes behavioral responses to expected future eligibility; public knowledge of the rules does not itself establish comparability.

The assignment diagnostics show little association between successive reservations in Rajasthan's earliest transition and substantial negative association in later transitions; UP also shows dependence across elections (Appendix Tables~\ref{tab:treat_corr}--\ref{tab:chi_sq_up}). These patterns describe the reservation histories available for comparison. Dependence does not by itself rule out a constrained lottery, and a failure to reject independence does not establish random assignment. We use district restrictions based on these tests as sensitivity analyses, reporting their consequences alongside the corresponding electoral estimates.

Balance tests in the open-election samples jointly assess the five Census covariates used in the adjusted models. Conditional on block fixed effects, these tests do not reject balance in any Rajasthan transition but reject it in all three UP transitions (Table~\ref{tab:open_sample_balance}). They cover the subset with complete Census data. The broader Census tests, which include GPs reserved at follow-up and additional characteristics, reject balance for Rajasthan's first transition ($p=\broadRajFirstP{}$) and all three UP transitions (Tables~\ref{tab:balance_raj_2005_2010} and \ref{tab:balance_up_2005_2010}). Prior-outcome tests ask whether future reservation predicts women's earlier electoral success among seats open in that earlier election; some estimates differ from zero (Table~\ref{tab:placebo_combined}). Together with the assignment rules, these findings motivate the sensitivity analyses below and warrant greater caution in interpreting the UP comparisons causally.

\subsection{Estimation and inference}\label{sec:estimation}

For each adjacent-election pair, we estimate the linear probability model
\begin{equation}\label{eq:estimation_eqn}
\text{FemaleWinner}_{g,t+1} = \beta\,\text{Quota}_{g,t} + \lambda_d + \varepsilon_{g,t+1},
\end{equation}
\noindent among GPs open in $t+1$. Here $g$ indexes GPs and $d$ indexes the outcome-year district--samiti in Rajasthan or district--block in UP. Our main estimates include fixed effects for these groups; we also report estimates without fixed effects and with the pre-treatment covariates described above. To distinguish adjustment from sample selection, we compare adjusted and unadjusted models on the same Census-complete observations. We also use treatment-year blocks and then add fixed effects for caste-reservation categories within those blocks. Standard errors cluster by block, using the corresponding year's identifiers. Under the identifying conditions above, $\beta$ captures the effect of prior quota assignment on women's probability of winning the following open election. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:short_term_bootstrap} reports wild cluster bootstrap inference. Throughout the paper, rates are reported as percentages and effects on those rates, including standard errors and confidence intervals, as percentage points. Counts and other non-rate outcomes retain their stated units.

We summarize the next-election effects by giving equal weight to each state and to each of its three election transitions. We estimate their joint covariance by clustering on geographic blocks harmonized across elections, retaining each comparison's original sample and fixed effects. Appendix~\ref{sec:pooling} details the calculation and alternatives for weighting and inference.

For duration comparison $a$ and follow-up $k$, we estimate
\begin{equation}\label{eq:duration}
\text{FemaleWinner}_{g,t(a,k)} = \theta_{a,k}\,\text{Exposed}_{g,a}
+ \lambda_{d,a,k} + \varepsilon_{g,a,k}.
\end{equation}
\noindent Here $\text{Exposed}_{g,a}$ identifies the specified reservation history rather than the comparison history, and $t(a,k)$ is the $k$th consecutive open election after reservation ends. These models use fixed 2005 block groups for both fixed effects and clustering, keeping the GPs and within-block weights constant across follow-ups. We test changes, $\theta_{a,k}-\theta_{a,1}$, using paired outcomes from the same GPs. Appendix~\ref{sec:persistence_horizons} gives the samples, weights, bootstrap inference, and adjustment for multiple temporal comparisons.

The additional-term models regress winning in the final open election on reservation in each of the three prior elections and all interactions, with outcome-year block fixed effects and clustering. Linear combinations of coefficients compare the specified histories, with uncertainty calculated from their joint covariance matrix. We report the numbers of GPs in each history and blocks containing both histories alongside the contrasts. The candidacy analyses use the same full-history specification for Rajasthan in 2020. Sensitivity analyses and precision assessments accompany each set of results.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Women\textquotesingle{}s Electoral Success after Reservation Ends}

Prior reservation produces a small average gain in women's probability of winning the next open election. Giving equal weight to each state and each of its three election transitions yields an estimated increase of \poolEstimate{} percentage points (95\% interval [\poolLow{}, \poolHigh{}]), against an equally weighted non-quota win rate of \poolControl{}\%. This average summarizes the two states and observed transitions; it does not assume a common effect across them.

\paragraph{Sensitivity.}\label{sec:robustness}
The estimated average remains modest under alternative weights and sample composition. Weighting the six estimates by their analysis sample sizes gives \poolSampleWeighted{} percentage points. Omitting one state--election comparison at a time, while retaining equal weights for the two states, gives averages from \poolOmitMin{} to \poolOmitMax{} points. District clustering and a joint wild multiplier bootstrap give similar intervals to the primary calculation (Table~\ref{tab:short_term_pooled}). The positive average comes from UP: its state average is \poolUp{} points, compared with \poolRaj{} in Rajasthan. Pooling therefore summarizes variation rather than establishing gains in both states.

Adjusting for the five Census characteristics changes individual estimates by at most \maxWithinSampleAdjustment{} points on the same observations. Independent UP election data also yield similar estimates. Restricting the sample to districts where tests do not detect dependence between successive quota assignments changes some comparisons and leaves no eligible districts for UP's final transition. Appendix~\ref{sec:app_next_election} reports these checks. They do not resolve the allocation and balance concerns discussed in Section~\ref{sec:identification}.

Published next-election estimates from West Bengal are also small or imprecise \citep{beaman2009powerful}. Appendix~\ref{sec:additional_evidence} presents these estimates alongside the urban evidence and examines sensitivity to which settings enter the synthesis.

\subsection{Additional Reserved Terms}\label{sec:additional_terms}

Longer reservation could produce larger gains by giving voters more experience of women leaders and women more time to build political careers. Adding a second reserved term yields small, imprecise estimated increments: \twoTermExtraMin{} to \twoTermExtraMax{} percentage points across the two states and timings, with every 95\% interval including zero (Table~\ref{tab:additional_term_main}). These comparisons add reservation in either 2005 or 2010 to reservation in 2015, holding the most recent reserved term and the final open election fixed.

Three reserved terms rather than one yield larger full-sample estimates: \rajFullExtra{} points in Rajasthan and \upFullExtra{} in UP. These gains are sensitive to which districts support the comparison. When we limit inference to districts where tests do not detect dependence between quota assignments in the first two transitions, the estimates fall to \rajRestrictedExtra{} points in Rajasthan (95\% interval [\rajRestrictedExtraLow{}, \rajRestrictedExtraHigh{}]) and \upRestrictedExtra{} in UP ([\upRestrictedExtraLow{}, \upRestrictedExtraHigh{}]). The intervals include zero and substantial positive gains. Together, these results do not establish that extending reservation reliably produces larger electoral gains. Appendix~\ref{sec:app_additional_terms} details the restrictions and the separate comparison of three reserved terms with no observed reservation.

\subsection{Persistence across Later Open Elections}\label{sec:temporal_persistence}

Electoral gains could persist, fade, or emerge only after several open elections. To distinguish these possibilities, we follow GPs reserved in 2005 and open thereafter, comparing them with GPs open in all four observed elections. The same GPs and block weights contribute to each follow-up. Requiring three consecutive open elections and observed outcomes throughout leaves smaller samples than the next-election analysis: \persistRajFirstN{} GPs in Rajasthan and \persistUpFirstN{} in UP.

The estimates show no consistent growth or decay (Figure~\ref{fig:persistence_main}). UP's estimated differences remain small across all three open elections, while Rajasthan's trajectory is less precisely estimated. From the first to the third open election, the estimated change is \persistUpChange{} percentage points in UP (95\% interval \persistUpChangeInterval{}) and \persistRajChange{} in Rajasthan (\persistRajChangeInterval{}). These tests account for repeated observations of the same GPs. They do not establish that the differences remain constant, and Rajasthan's interval permits substantial changes in either direction.

\paragraph{Sensitivity.}
Relaxing these follow-up requirements leaves UP's estimates small but changes Rajasthan's trajectory. Allowing the sample to vary across follow-ups also changes the GPs being compared, so those estimates cannot establish how a difference evolves for a fixed group. Other reservation histories show larger changes in Rajasthan, without establishing a common pattern across histories or states. Appendix~\ref{sec:persistence_horizons} reports these comparisons, direct tests of changes, and sensitivity to individual blocks.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Women\textquotesingle{}s Candidacy}\label{sec:candidacy_results}

Quotas could encourage more women to run without substantially increasing their chances of winning \citep{o2020can, auerbach2016electoral}. Rajasthan's 2020 candidate records provide little evidence of such an increase. Comparing seats reserved only in 2015 with seats unreserved in all three observed prior elections, the estimated effect on women's share of candidates is \candShare{} percentage points (95\% interval [\candShareLow{}, \candShareHigh{}]). The estimated effect on whether any woman contests is \candAny{} points [\candAnyLow{}, \candAnyHigh{}], and the estimated change in the number of women candidates is approximately zero [\candCountLow{}, \candCountHigh{}] per GP. All comparisons concern seats open to women and men in 2020 (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:candidacy_combined}).

The estimated increase in votes received by women among the winner and runner-up is \candVotes{} percentage points [\candVotesLow{}, \candVotesHigh{}], measured as a share of all valid votes. This measure excludes votes for women finishing below second place. The estimates therefore provide no clear evidence of increased candidacy or electoral support, although their uncertainty permits meaningful gains.

\subsection{Evidence across Rural and Urban Settings}\label{sec:cross_setting_results}

The broader evidence also points to modest average gains after reservation ends. Combining next-election comparisons from Rajasthan, UP, West Bengal, Mumbai, Delhi, Chandigarh, and Bengaluru gives a pooled estimate of \appMetaEstimate{} percentage points (95\% interval \appMetaInterval{}). We first average repeated election comparisons within each setting, then combine the seven setting averages using a random-effects model. Omitting one setting at a time yields estimates between \appMetaOmitLow{} and \appMetaOmitHigh{} points, although their precision varies substantially. Appendix~\ref{sec:additional_evidence} presents the estimates and sensitivity analyses.

We find no clear rural--urban difference. The fitted persistence contrasts are \appRuralMean{} percentage points in Rajasthan, UP, and West Bengal and \appUrbanMean{} points in Mumbai, Delhi, Chandigarh, and Bengaluru. The rural-minus-urban difference is \appRuralGap{} points (95\% interval \appRuralGapInterval{}; $p=\appRuralGapP{}$). Using equal or covariance-adjusted precision weights and varying assumptions about cross-election dependence yields differences from $\appRuralGapPoolingLow{}$ to $\appRuralGapPoolingHigh{}$ points; every interval includes zero (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:additional_pooling_methods}).

The urban comparisons vary: Bengaluru yields \appBengaluruEstimate{} points, and Chandigarh's later election yields \appChandigarhLaterEstimate{} points, both imprecisely estimated. Omitting Bengaluru gives a rural--urban difference of \appRuralGapOmitBengaluru{} points; omitting Mumbai gives \appRuralGapOmitMumbai{} points. Both intervals include zero. Differences in office type, reservation rules, and election periods also prevent these comparisons from isolating the role of urbanization.

\paragraph{Development within rural settings.}\label{sec:het_effects}
Within Rajasthan and UP, we likewise find no clear evidence that quota effects are larger in more developed villages. We examine differences by proximity to towns, infrastructure availability, and female literacy, using measures from the 2001 Census. The estimates do not establish a consistent relationship between development and subsequent electoral gains, but their uncertainty permits meaningful differences. Appendix~\ref{sec:app_het} reports these comparisons and sensitivity to the distance measure.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Officeholding and Political Careers}\label{sec:mechanisms}

Quotas could generate lasting electoral gains by giving women opportunities to demonstrate their abilities and acquire political experience. Those opportunities may be limited when male relatives act on their behalf. We examine who handles contact directed to elected representatives, the characteristics of quota and non-quota officeholders, and whether they contest another election.

\subsection{Male Proxy Governance}\label{sec:phone_audit}

Direct engagement with constituents gives women opportunities to demonstrate their abilities as representatives. In 2024, we conducted a phone audit of Rajasthan's elected village heads using the telephone numbers recorded in their 2020 election filings.\footnote{The study protocol was reviewed by the IRB under protocol number UP-24-00498.} We sampled \phoneQuotaSampled{} representatives from women-reserved seats and \phoneOpenSampled{} from non-quota seats, including seats won by women. Interviewers asked to speak with the elected representative and made up to three attempts.

Among answered contacts, we reached the elected officeholder in \phoneQuotaMemberPct{}\% of quota seats, compared with \phoneOpenMemberPct{}\% of non-quota seats (Table~\ref{tab:full_survey}). These figures include contact through transfers and callbacks. Calls were answered for \phoneQuotaAnswered{} quota-seat representatives and \phoneOpenAnswered{} non-quota representatives, giving response rates of \phoneQuotaResponsePct{}\% and \phoneOpenResponsePct{}\%, respectively.

Male relatives frequently handled calls directed to quota-elected women, and recorded transfers to the officeholder were rare (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:phone_detail}). In one case, the elected representative answered but passed the call to her husband when questions about panchayat governance began. In another, a father-in-law identified himself as the representative. When relatives handle these interactions, voters have fewer opportunities to observe women's leadership, and women have fewer opportunities to develop experience through direct engagement. These patterns complement evidence on male household members as constraints on women's political participation \citep{cheema2020canvassing, khan2021count, prillaman2023strength, kumar2024}.

The contact gap survives uncertainty about respondent identity. Assigning unresolved cases alternatively to officeholders or other respondents gives contact rates of \phoneQuotaLower{}--\phoneQuotaUpper{}\% in quota seats and \phoneOpenLower{}--\phoneOpenUpper{}\% in non-quota seats (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:phone_identity_sensitivity}). Telephone access and household practices may contribute to this gap. The audit documents who handles calls to the listed number; it does not measure women's participation in meetings or decisions over spending.

\subsection{Candidate Characteristics}\label{sec:cand_char}

Opportunities to demonstrate leadership may also depend on the resources women bring to office. Education, financial resources, and experience outside the household could help representatives navigate government and act independently of relatives. Differences in these characteristics may therefore matter for whether reserved terms allow women to establish themselves as political leaders.

In Rajasthan's 2020 election, quota-seat winners are younger, less educated, and report fewer assets than non-quota winners, including women (Table~\ref{tab:main_cand_char}). Their average age is \allAgeQuota{} years, compared with \allAgeOpen{} in non-quota seats; \allGraduateQuota{}\% are graduates, compared with \allGraduateOpen{}\%. Mean log assets are \allLogassetsQuota{} versus \allLogassetsOpen{}, and \allUnemployedQuota{}\% report being unemployed, compared with \allUnemployedOpen{}\%. The candidate pool shows differences in the same direction. UP's winner records also show lower average age and education in quota seats across three elections (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:up_cand_char}).

\input{../tabs/cand_characteristics_combined.tex}

These comparisons describe the people elected under the two reservation regimes. They do not establish differences in governing ability, and reported unemployment need not imply an absence of work or relevant experience. The overall gaps also reflect the different sex composition of quota and non-quota winners. Among women winners in Rajasthan, graduation rates and mean log assets are similar across the two groups: \womenGraduateQuota{}\% versus \womenGraduateOpen{}\%, and \womenLogassetsQuota{} in both. Women winning quota seats remain younger and more likely to report being unemployed (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:candidate_women}). The evidence thus identifies differences in the resources officeholders bring to the role, while leaving open how those resources affect their independence and subsequent political careers.

\subsection{Contesting Again}\label{sec:recontesting_results}

Quotas could produce lasting representation by helping women build political careers that continue after reservation ends. Yet quota-seat winners are less likely than non-quota winners to contest the next election in the same GP. We follow 2015 winners to Rajasthan's 2020 election and UP's 2021 election, restricting both samples to seats subsequently reserved for neither women nor a caste group. This ensures that the new reservation category does not prevent prior winners from running. The comparison includes all prior non-quota winners, including women.

Under strict name matching, \recontestRajQuotaRate{}\% of quota winners reappear as candidates in Rajasthan, compared with \recontestRajNonquotaRate{}\% of non-quota winners. The corresponding rates in UP are \recontestUpQuotaRate{}\% and \recontestUpNonquotaRate{}\%. Adjusting for prior caste reservation and block fixed effects gives quota-minus-non-quota differences of \recontestRajDifference{} percentage points in Rajasthan (95\% interval [\recontestRajLow{}, \recontestRajHigh{}]) and \recontestUpDifference{} points in UP [\recontestUpLow{}, \recontestUpHigh{}]. More generous matching raises the rates to \recontestRajGenerousQuotaRate{}\% versus \recontestRajGenerousNonquotaRate{}\% in Rajasthan and \recontestUpGenerousQuotaRate{}\% versus \recontestUpGenerousNonquotaRate{}\% in UP. The levels are sensitive to how names are matched, but quota winners return less often under every rule (Appendix~\ref{sec:recontesting}).

These comparisons suggest that quota winners less often convert a reserved term into continued electoral participation in the same office. This could reflect quotas bringing less politically established women into office, fewer opportunities to build a career while serving, or both. The comparisons do not distinguish those explanations. They also do not capture candidacy elsewhere or the entry of other women.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Quotas secure women's representation while seats are reserved, but the electoral gains after reservation ends are modest. Across Rajasthan and UP, the average next-election gain is \poolEstimate{} percentage points, against a non-quota win rate of \poolControl{}\%. Additional reserved terms do not reliably produce larger gains, and following the same places through later open elections reveals no consistent growth or decay. The broader evidence also points to modest average gains, without establishing that rural effects are smaller than urban effects.

These findings qualify the expectation that electing women will itself generate a growing pool of experienced candidates and greater voter support. In our phone audit, we reached the officeholder in \phoneQuotaMemberWhole{}\% of quota seats with an answered call, compared with \phoneOpenMemberWhole{}\% of non-quota seats; male relatives frequently handled calls directed to women. Quota winners also entered office with fewer educational and financial resources and were less likely to contest the next election in the same GP. Together, these patterns suggest that access to office may provide limited opportunities to demonstrate leadership and build an independent political career.

The democratic value of women's representation does not depend on quotas eventually becoming unnecessary. But the expectation that reserved terms will generate substantial electoral gains afterward requires more than women's presence in office: it requires opportunities to exercise authority, gain recognition, and continue in politics.
