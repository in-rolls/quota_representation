# Evidence for the theoretical framework

This is an argument plan, not a systematic review. The manuscript now follows this eight-paragraph structure; the proposed paragraph below records the preceding discussion. Source versions, checked pages, and limits are in `studies/`; local PDFs and hashes are registered in `papers.csv`.

1. **Women governing can change beliefs about competence.** Use Beaman et al. (2009) for the distinction between competence beliefs and taste-based prejudice. Observing performance and attributing it to a woman are possible links between holding office and later electoral support. Public-goods effects in Chattopadhyay–Duflo establish policy consequences, not the attribution mechanism itself.
2. **Exposure can challenge prejudice and expand aspirations.** Keep taste-based discrimination separate from statistical discrimination. Beaman et al. (2012) concerns girls' aspirations and schooling; it does not directly establish adult candidate entry. Do not convert a larger repeated-exposure point estimate into a demonstrated dose response.
3. **Office can expand political experience and organizations.** Goyal (2024) supplies a concrete organizational channel: recruitment of women party activists. Ban–Rao and Afridi et al. connect experience to governing performance. Distinguish experience acquired by a person from repeated reservation of a seat, which may elect different women. Urban party organization need not carry over unchanged to rural elections.
4. **Formal office need not confer control or public credit.** Develop the sarpanch-pati argument around who makes decisions, interacts with citizens and officials, and receives credit. These dimensions can diverge. Beaman et al. (2011), pp. 160–161, explicitly explains why spousal assistance need not eliminate women's influence on policy or citizens' expression of preferences. Replace the current categorical claim that proxy governance severs every channel.
5. **Preparation and resources can affect governing opportunities.** Gajwani–Zhang (2015), pp. 246–249, measures knowledge of government rules and official contact directly, with differences persisting after adjustment for schooling. Their public-goods results concern schools and roads requiring coordination. Ban–Rao shows that deficits relative to non-quota winners can coexist with positive selection relative to eligible women. Sood's qualifications draft extends the comparison across offices and attributes. Age, schooling and wealth are separate attributes; younger age alone does not demonstrate lower effectiveness.
6. **Distinguish electoral implications.** The next open election, later open elections after quotas end, and additional reserved terms answer different questions. Learning and organizational development could accumulate, while restricted authority could limit accumulation. Neither mechanism requires a linear dose response. Within-term learning evidence is not itself evidence about repeated quota assignments.
7. **Explain why electing women may produce limited gains for future women candidates.** If male relatives make decisions and receive credit, a reserved term may offer less evidence of women's competence, weaker exemplars, less opportunity to express women's policy priorities, and less independent political experience. Limited administrative preparation or resources may further constrain delivery and autonomy. This motivates examining access to officeholders and their characteristics. The phone audit measures whom callers reach, not control of all decisions; candidate records measure attributes, not performance. Present these as evidence relevant to the conditions for the mechanisms, without claiming an identified mediation effect.
8. **Explain contextual variation conditionally.** Ban–Rao emphasizes experience and institutional context; Gajwani–Zhang documents constraints in Tamil Nadu. The qualifications draft finds differences across offices and settings. Development may affect autonomy, networks and baseline opportunities, but a rural–urban comparison does not isolate development and its sign is not mechanically determined.

## Suggested paragraph 7

Electing women may do less to improve other women's electoral prospects when office gives them limited opportunities to demonstrate competence and build political experience. If male relatives perform the public role and receive credit, voters have less opportunity to revise their beliefs about women leaders, and potential candidates see fewer examples of women exercising authority. Constraints on administrative knowledge and access to officials may also limit what women can accomplish in office (Gajwani and Zhang 2015). These obstacles could weaken the learning, exemplar, and experience channels through which quotas are expected to help women win subsequent elections. Our phone audit and comparisons of officeholder characteristics help assess whether these obstacles may explain the limited electoral gains.

The earlier paragraph on authority should retain Beaman et al.'s qualification about policy influence despite spousal involvement, so paragraph 7 need not repeat it. Do not add a claim that younger officeholders are necessarily less effective. The qualifications paper can support broader context, but its overlapping Rajasthan/UP records are not independent confirmation of this paper's findings.

The central question remains the effect of electing women on subsequent women’s electoral success. Authority and preparation explain possible limits to that process; they are not a replacement research question. Cite the qualifications draft in the earlier preparation paragraph, where its comparison across settings contributes directly.
